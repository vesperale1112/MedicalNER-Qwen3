1 The Integrated Biomedical Databases
In this part, we delve into a comprehensive exploration of a wide array of existing
biomedical databases. These databases are thoughtfully categorized into chemical-
centric, pathway-centric, gene-centric, and Phenotype/disease-centric databases.
1. Chemical-centric database
• Side Effect Resource (SIDER)[1]: contains information on marketed medicines
and their recorded adverse drug reactions. The information is extracted from public
documents and package inserts. The available information includes side effect fre-
quency, drug, and side effect classifications, as well as links to further information,
for example, drug–target relations.
• DrugBank[2]: a bioinformatics and cheminformatics knowledge base with rich
drug data information (i.e., chemical, pharmacological, and pharmaceutical) and
comprehensive drug target information (i.e., sequence, structure, and pathway).
• Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD)[3]: a regularly updated,
chemical-centric resource that curates over 3.4 million evidence-based interactions
involving chemicals, genes, phenotypes, anatomy, diseases, and exposure across
multiple species, drawn from chemical-centric manually curated articles.
2. Pathway-centric database
• Reactome[4]: provides manually curated molecular details across various physio-
logical and pathological biological processes in humans, including hereditary and
acquired disease processes.
3. Gene-centric database
• Gene Ontology (GO)[5] [6]: focuses on defining gene activity on the molecular
level, linking genes to locations in the body where its function is performed, and
establishing links between genes and biological processes.
• Bgee[7]: a database for retrieving and comparing gene expression patterns across
multiple animal species. It provides an intuitive answer to the question ”Where
is a gene expressed?” and supports cancer, agriculture, and evolutionary biology
research.
• ClinVar[8]: a public repository that collects, stores, and displays information on
human genetic variants and their observed relationships to phenotypes, including
clinical significance assertions, supportive evidence, and review status, with data
sourced from clinical testing, research, and literature reports.
4. Phenotype/diseases-centric database
• DisGeNET[9]: one of the largest available collections of genes and variants
involved in human diseases. It integrates data from expert-curated repositories,
GWAS catalogues[10], animal models, and the scientific literature. DisGeNET
data are homogeneously annotated with controlled vocabularies and community-
driven ontologies. Additionally, several original metrics are provided to assist the
prioritization of genotype–phenotype relationships.

--- PAGE 3 ---
• Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO)[11]: a structured database of over 13,000
medically relevant phenotypes and disease-phenotype annotations, derived from
clinical data. Its structure is a directed acyclic graph connected by is-a (subclass-of)
relationships.
• Mondo Disease Ontology (MONDO)[12]: a semi-automatically constructed
ontology that merges multiple disease resources to yield a coherent merged ontology.
Unlike generic ontology mappings, MONDO employs rigorous semantics to distin-
guish when two disease names or identifiers are exactly equivalent versus merely
being related. This meticulous distinction ensures a high level of clarity and precision
in disease representation and inter-resource mappings.
5. Anatomy-centric database
• Uberon[13]: an integrated cross-species ontology consisting of over 6,500 classes rep-
resenting a variety of anatomical entities, organized according to traditional anatom-
ical classification criteria. The ontology represents structures in a species-neutral
way and includes extensive associations to existing species-centric anatomical
ontologies, allowing the integration of model organism and human data.
6. The Unified Medical Language System (UMLS)[14]: designed by the US
National Library of Medicine, is a key resource that includes a Metathesaurus with
controlled biomedical vocabularies. Concepts within this Metathesaurus are sorted into
127 semantic types, such as organisms and chemicals, linked by 54 relationships like the
hierarchical ”isa”. The semantic network catalogs these and includes five major non-
hierarchical relationship categories, capturing potential connections between instances
of different types. UMLS aids in medical informatics with natural language processing,
free access, and quarterly updates.
2 Entity and Relationship Types
Through an in-depth analysis of high-impact PubMed articles, we identify ten key
themes in mental health research, focusing on prevalent mental disorders like depres-
sion, anxiety, and schizophrenia. These themes include pathogenesis, pharmacological
interventions, therapeutic approaches, health-related factors, neurotransmitter and
neural signaling dynamics, neural development and neurobiology, advances in neu-
roimaging and neurophysiology, genetic influences and polymorphisms, intersections of
endocrinology and metabolic processes, along with immunological and inflammatory
responses. We also pinpoint nine entity types (Health Factors, Anatomy, Phenotype,
Sign, Method/Procedure, Physiology, Gene/Protein, Disease/Symptom, Drug/Chem-
ical) and seven relationship types (Risk relationships, Diagnostic relationships,
Characteristic relationships, Association relationships, Therapeutic relationships,
Hierarchical relationships, and Abbreviation) that are central to mental disorders
research. These categories offer a structured lens for examining the complexities of
mental health, thereby enhancing the comprehensiveness of our MDKG.

2.1 Entity Types
1. Health Factors: This refers to factors that influence a person’s likelihood of devel-
oping health issues, such as health behaviors, clinical care, social and economic
factors, and physical environment factors.
2. Anatomy: This refers to bodily structures, including organs, tissues, cells, and
other components within living organisms.
3. Phenotype: This refers to observable or measurable changes in an individual’s
anatomy, physiology, or signs.
4. Sign: This refers to measurements or indicators of the body or organ’s functions
or states, often obtained through laboratory tests or examinations.
5. Method/Procedure: This refers to techniques or procedures used in research,
diagnosis, treatment, or prevention of diseases, as well as disciplines and occupa-
tions related to the biomedical field.
6. Physiology: This refers to biological, molecular, and cellular functions, including
mental processes and organ or tissue functions.
7. Gene/Protein: This refers to a segment of DNA that carries genetic information
involved in the sequencing of molecules and the formation of proteins, enzymes,
and receptors.
8. Disease/Symptom: This refers to various types of abnormalities or dysfunctions,
including congenital, acquired, injuries, pathologies, mental or behavioral issues,
and cellular or molecular issues.
9. Drug/Chemical: This refers to substances with specific structures and func-
tions, including organic and inorganic compounds, pharmacological substances,
hormones, and hazardous materials.
2.2 Relationship Types
1. Risk relationship: This relationship refers to a factor that increases the likelihood
of developing a specific disease or a factor that exacerbates the progression of a
specific disease. For instance, poor dietary habits could increase the risk of heart
disease, while smoking could exacerbate lung cancer progression.
2. Diagnostic relationship: The diagnostic relationships in our knowledge graph
are categorized into two main types. The first type encompasses disease diagnosis
methods, which include tools and techniques like psychological assessments, neu-
roimaging technologies, and biochemical tests. The second type involves identifying
specific features or biomarkers that assist physicians or researchers in diagnosing
particular diseases or pathological conditions. These features or biomarkers are
typically characterized by their high diagnostic specificity and sensitivity, enabling
effective differentiation between healthy and diseased states or among various
disease states.
3. Characteristic relationship: Characteristic relationships in the graph describe
the symptoms, clinical manifestations, or distinct features of a disease. These
characteristics span multiple dimensions, including physiological, biochemical,
behavioral, and genetic levels. For example, in the context of depression, character-
istic relationships might encompass symptoms like persistent sadness, biochemical

factors such as serotonin imbalance, behavioral signs like loss of interest in activities,
and genetic predispositions.
4. Association relationship: This relationship describes an association or connec-
tion between two or more entities, where changes in one entity may correspond
with changes in another but not necessarily imply a cause-and-effect relationship.
5. Therapeutic relationship: This relationship type highlights the link between various
treatments or interventions and their impact on a specific disease or condition.
It includes a range of treatment methods like medication, psychotherapy, lifestyle
modifications, surgical procedures, and others.
6. Hierarchical relationship: This relationship refers to the classification and hier-
archical structure within the same entity type. For instance, within the ’Disease’
category, major classifications like ’mental disorders’ could be further divided into
subclass diseases such as depression, anxiety, and schizophrenia.
7. Abbreviation relationship: This relationship type refers to the use of abbre-
viations or acronyms to represent longer terms or phrases, especially in medical
or technical fields. For example, ’AIDS’ is an abbreviation for ’Acquired Immune
Deficiency Syndrome’, and ’MRI’ stands for ’Magnetic Resonance Imaging’.
3 Confidence Score Calculation
The confidence score integrates multiple quality indicators of scientific statements
(triplets) using a weighted framework. Each component score is normalized to [0,1]
and scaled by a predefined weight, ensuring the final score remains within the range
[0,3]. Prior research on assessing study quality has highlighted key factors influencing
a study’s credibility and impact, including impact factor, statement frequency, and the
number of distinct publication years[15–17]. Our framework incorporates these estab-
lished factors and further refines their contributions based on insights from domain
experts, ensuring a balanced and context-aware evaluation. The confidence score is
computed as follows:
Sconfidence = Sprediction + 0.8 · SIF + 0.6 · SF requency + 0.4 · Syears + 0.2 · Sdate
−0.5 · Scontradiction
• Model Prediction Score (Sprediction): Quantifies the base trustworthiness of the
triplet generated by the prediction model. The model directly outputs the score,
ranging from [0, 1].
• Impact Factor Score (SIF ): Reflects the academic influence of the resource.
SIF (i) = 1 −exp (−αi)
where i is the average impact factor of the source journal. To align with the 75th
percentile impact factor value (6.6), the decay coefficient α = 0.25 ensures SIF ≥0.8
when i ≥6.6.
• Frequency Score (SF requency): Measures how frequently the triplet appears across
different resources.
SF requency(n) = 1 −exp (α(n −1))

Syears(nt) = 1 −exp (−αnt)
where nt is the number of distinct publication years. For the 75th percentile nt =

In summary, the confidence score aggregates multiple aspects of a resource’s quality
and relevance, with each factor having a distinct role in shaping the final score. The
decay functions ensure that the score is adjusted for diminishing returns, while the
weights reflect the relative importance of each factor in the overall assessment. This
approach allows for a nuanced evaluation of resources, balancing credibility, academic
impact, historical significance, recency, and frequency of use. The distribution of con-

4 Additional Details of Information Extraction
4.1 Performance of Relationship Annotation with GPT-4
We used GPT-4 for pre-annotation, significantly reducing the time and effort required
for manual annotation. GPT-4 performed two main tasks: (1) identifying noun phrases
in abstracts and (2) determining whether a specific relationship (e.g., therapeutic or
risk) existed between annotated entity pairs. The second task was treated as a binary
classification problem.
We evaluated GPT-4’s performance by comparing its annotations with manually
reviewed data from 500 mental health-related abstracts (MDIEC). For entity identi-
fication, GPT-4 achieved an F1-score of 0.88, with a precision of 0.84 and a recall of
0.93, demonstrating strong recognition of relevant terms while occasionally including
non-essential ones. For relationship extraction, performance was assessed against the
manually reviewed dataset, with results summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

4.2 Performance of Triplet Extraction with Spert.pl and
Refinement with GPT-4o
Triplet extraction was performed in two stages. First, a BERT-based classification
model (Spert.pl) was fine-tuned on the MDIEC annotated dataset for named entity

--- PAGE 8 ---
recognition (NER) and relationship extraction (RE) and then applied to unannotated
abstracts to extract triplets. In the second stage, GPT-4o was used to validate and
refine the extracted triplets, improving their accuracy and reliability.
1. The performance of Triplet Extraction
For the NER task, Spert.pl performed multi-class classification to identify and cat-
egorize potential entities. For the RE task, the model determined whether a specific
relationship existed between a given pair of entities. As shown in Supplementary
Table 2, Spert.pl achieved strong performance in NER (macro F1-score: 0.88), but
relationship extraction remained challenging (macro F1-score: 0.67). When eval-
uating the joint NER and RE performance—assessing whether both entities and
their relationships were correctly identified—the model achieved a macro F1-score
of 0.63.
2. The performance of Triplet Validation
To enhance the accuracy of extracted triplets, GPT-4o was employed for valida-
tion and refinement. After this process, the micro F1-score for triplet extraction
improved to 0.79, with micro precision increasing to 0.84. This substantial improve-
ment significantly enhanced the quality of the knowledge graph while maintaining
cost efficiency.

4.3 Performance of Table Information Extraction
We have conducted a manual review to assess the performance of extracting base-
line characteristics from PDF tables. This review involved evaluating the accuracy of
the process and identifying the causes of any errors encountered. The results of this
evaluation are presented in Supplementary Table 3. Out of the 302 total documents
reviewed, 238 were PDFs, among which 72 contained target tables expected to be
extracted for analysis.
The review identified two primary types of errors. The first, Tables Not Found,
occurred in 12 documents. This issue was primarily caused by two factors: (1) in two
instances, the system failed to detect tables because their titles spanned multiple pages,
making it difficult to correctly associate the title with the corresponding data; (2) in
three cases, the system struggled to identify tables due to unclear or missing keywords
(in our method, we differentiate tables by locating baseline-related keywords). The
remaining seven cases were attributed to less identifiable reasons, likely related to
complex formatting or irregular data layouts.

4.4 Comparative Analysis of Large Language Models for
Biomedical Information Extraction
We systematically evaluated the performance of four language models—GPT-4
(gpt-4), GPT-3 (text-davinci-003), Claude-3-opus, and ChatGPT (GPT-3.5-turbo-
1106)—on two key biomedical natural language processing (NLP) tasks: named entity
recognition (NER) and relationship extraction (RE). Both tasks were formulated as
multi-class classification problems. To benchmark performance, we used MDIEC, a
dataset of 500 manually annotated medical abstracts from our institutional corpus.
The dataset included nine entity types (Anatomy, Disease/Symptom, Drug/Chemical,
Gene/Protein, Health Factors, Method/Procedure, Phenotype, Physiology, and Sign)
and seven relationship types (Therapeutic, Diagnostic, Risk, Characteristic, Associa-
tion, Hierarchical, and Abbreviation). A standardized few-shot learning framework was
applied, and model performance was evaluated using precision, recall, and F1-score
metrics.
For NER (Supplementary Table 4), Claude-3-opus demonstrated marginally
superior performance to GPT-4 (F1: 0.87 vs. 0.85). In relationship extraction (Supple-
mentary Table 5), GPT-4 achieved superior performance (F1: 0.66 vs. Claude-3-opus:
0.63). Both models significantly outperformed GPT-3.5-turbo (NER F1: 0.74; RE F1:
0.55) and GPT-3 (NER F1: 0.63; RE F1: 0.51) across all metrics.
